This page describes how the native engines (epoll and io_uring, Linux only) place their event loops on CPUs. The std engine pins nothing. The README carries a short summary; the rules below are the full text, including what each was measured on and where it does not apply. Several rules were fitted to one arm64 big.LITTLE host after its data had been seen, and the page says so where it matters.

## Pinning

Loop `i` pins its thread to the `i`-th CPU the thread that starts the server is allowed to use (`taskset`, a cgroup cpuset, `sched_setaffinity`), never to a CPU outside that set; on a host with several NUMA nodes the CPUs are taken from the nodes the set covers in turn, so every allowed CPU holds a loop before any holds a second.

## Default loop count on hosts whose CPUs are alike

With `Workers` unset on a host whose allowed CPUs are alike, the engines start `GOMAXPROCS - max(1, GOMAXPROCS/8)` loops (28 of 32, 14 of 16, 7 of 8, 3 of 4; no reserve for a `GOMAXPROCS` of 2 or less): a loop can hold its thread and its P while it blocks in the kernel, so the Ps without a loop are left to the goroutines that are not loops (async and WebSocket handlers, the HTTP/2 stream pool, the garbage collector's workers) (celeris#756, #757). The mechanism is inferred from traces, not established; the reserve is provisional and its effect on latency is still being measured. To opt out, set `Workers: runtime.GOMAXPROCS(0)`: on such a host it starts one loop per P, as before (on a big.LITTLE host the default is not reduced, and a `Workers` above the non-little CPUs wraps loops over them, see below); a `Workers` you set is never reduced. `Metrics.Workers`, `NumWorkers()` and the server-wide `Config.MaxConns` limit follow the loops started, as on big.LITTLE below.

## big.LITTLE hosts

On a host whose allowed CPUs differ in capacity (arm64 big.LITTLE, read from `/sys/devices/system/cpu/cpuN/cpu_capacity`, or the CPU parts in `/proc/cpuinfo` when the kernel exports none), the engines start at most one loop per CPU with at least half the largest capacity (`min(GOMAXPROCS, non-little CPUs)`: 8 loops on a 12-CPU host with 4 little CPUs, fewer than `GOMAXPROCS`) and pin every one of them: none runs on a little CPU and none is left unpinned (celeris#1036; a loop that floated drifted onto the little CPUs and a loop pinned to one stalled its connections). A `Config.Workers` above that count is honoured: the extra loops wrap round-robin over the non-little CPUs, each pinned, so the CPUs that get a second loop (4 of 8 for `Workers: 12`) take about twice the connections of the others; on io_uring the count is still capped by `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK`. Consequences of the smaller count: `Metrics.Workers` and `NumWorkers()` read the loops started (on the adaptive engine, the sum over its two engines), driver pools sized from `NumWorkers()` default smaller (a PostgreSQL pool of `4*NumWorkers`: 32 on that host, not 48), and `Config.MaxConns` is per loop, so the server-wide limit shrinks with it. The measurements that motivated this plan were taken on earlier builds and are not quoted here; [#1036](https://github.com/goceleris/celeris/issues/1036) stays open until the plan is measured on the v1.6.0 release. The engine logs one line saying how many loops it started and why; a pin the kernel refuses is logged once.

## Leaving loops off the CPUs that service the NIC

With `Workers` unset, and a default count of at least one loop for each non-little CPU (8 of 8 on that host; a lower `GOMAXPROCS`, such as the default under a Kubernetes CPU limit, plans as above, so 7 default loops are 0, 1, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10, more than the 6 loops of 8), the default plan also leaves the loops off the non-little CPUs that service the busy interrupt vectors of the NIC port that received the traffic (celeris#1036 item 1). At start the engine takes the physical ports behind the listen address (through bonds and VLANs); if one of them took at least 60% of the packets received since boot (`/sys/class/net/<if>/statistics/rx_packets`, and at least a million) it leaves without a loop the non-little CPUs that service its vectors with at least 1000 interrupts since boot (`/proc/interrupts`, `/proc/irq/N/effective_affinity_list`): CPUs 6 and 8 on that host, so the loops sit on 0, 1, 7, 9, 10 and 11. It frees never more than a quarter of the non-little CPUs (the loops never fall under `max(min(2, n), ceil(3n/4))` for `n` non-little CPUs) and never raises the count. A CPU that also services a busy vector of another port (or one shared with another port) keeps its loop, and at least one busy vector of a port with at least 10% of the packets pooled over the listener's ports (a choice, not measured; the measured host's ports carry about 27% and 73%) must stay on a CPU that keeps its loop: with a single port, or with a data NIC beside a management NIC that carries less, the plan is the plan without the rule. The rule also does nothing when a file cannot be read, when a port has a million packets and no busy vector, when the busy vectors sit on more CPUs than the quarter allows (a NIC spread over its queues), when the ports share the packets about evenly, or when a busy vector of another port is serviced by a CPU that runs no loop (the measured host's boot placement, IRQ 167 on little CPU 4: that plan is not measured). A host whose CPUs are alike, and any `Config.Workers` you set, read no NIC file. The Info line of the plan names the CPUs left free, the port and its share; the Debug line names the candidates it passed over or why the rule stepped aside. On the boots of the one cluster host where it was measured the rule planned the loops 0, 1, 7, 9, 10 and 11 by itself; whether it engages depends on the host and on the boot, because the packet counts it reads are since boot. Its measured effect is not quoted here (see #1036).

### Limits of this rule

The rule is fitted to that one host after its data was seen (the 60% and the quarter are choices), and its counts are since boot, so a server started at boot steps aside and a wrapped 32-bit interrupt counter can hide a vector (IRQ 135 on the measured host is at about 4.05e9 of 4.29e9); a LAG rehash that moves the traffic to the other port leaves the plan on the old CPUs until the next start; a wildcard listener pools every physical port that is up, related or not (the 10% share above governs a data NIC beside a management NIC); on NICs whose ports share all their vectors (mlx4) the rule never acts; io_uring's `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK` cap keeps the first loops of the plan in CPU order and can drop the CPU the rule meant to keep (a memlock of 4 gives 0, 1, 7 and 9); and the adaptive engine builds its io_uring standby when it first switches, from the counts of that moment, so its loop set can differ from the primary's.

## Inline handlers inherit the pin

A handler that runs inline runs on a pinned thread and a process it starts inherits the pin: start it from a goroutine of its own, or mark the route `.Async()` (see `Config.AsyncHandlers`). Setting `AsyncHandlers` alone is not enough: a route that only inherits it runs inline until it is promoted.

## The NIC-interrupt warning

This section applies to the epoll and io_uring, Linux, hosts whose allowed CPUs differ in capacity only; a host whose CPUs are alike reads nothing there). Once the engine has started its loops it finds the CPUs that service the interrupt vectors of the network interface the listener is reachable through (celeris#1036) and logs one Warn when a serviced CPU of the allowed set runs no loop and is either a little CPU, or a big CPU while no vector it found has a loop beside it. It only reports: it adds no loop, moves no loop and moves no interrupt, so the loops, `Metrics.Workers` and `NumWorkers()` are the ones described above on every host. The rule behind the Warn is the premise of the rx-port rule above: a loop stays beside a busy vector. On the host measured, CPUs 6 and 8 service the vectors of enp49s0 and run no loop in the default plan, and that plan is the measured one as long as CPU 1 keeps a loop beside IRQ 167; so a big CPU without a loop is not a reason to warn while another vector has a loop beside it, and with `Workers: 4` or a `GOMAXPROCS` of 4 (loops on 0, 1, 6 and 7, CPU 8 without one) nothing is logged. A little CPU runs no loop by design, so a vector serviced by one is always named. The CPUs the rx-port rule left without a loop on purpose are never named: a vector it listed, on a CPU it freed. What the Warn does on that host:

### What the warning does on the measured host

- IRQ 167 on CPU 1, default `Workers` (the rule frees CPUs 6 and 8, loops on 0, 1, 7, 9, 10 and 11): no Warn.
- IRQ 167 on little CPU 4 (the placement after boot; the rule steps aside, eight loops): the Warn names CPU 4 and IRQ 167. This is the case it exists for. If the 32-bit counter of IRQ 167 has wrapped and reads small, the rule still sees the vector busy (1000 interrupts) and steps aside, and the Warn names it whatever its share.
- A `RLIMIT_MEMLOCK` cut of the io_uring loops: with a vector on a CPU that keeps a loop (IRQ 159 sits on CPU 0, which every cut keeps) no big CPU is named; with none, the CPU whose loop the cut dropped is.
- A vector of another port on a CPU the rule freed is named; the rule's own vector on it is not.

### How it differs from the rule above

The report differs from the rule in what it reads: it does not read `rx_packets`, so any vector it found counts as "beside a loop", where the rule asks for a port with at least 10% of the packets. The interface is the one that owns the bound IP, or every up, non-loopback interface with a global address for a wildcard listener, followed through bond, team, VLAN, macvlan and bridge `lower_*` links to the physical devices; their vectors come from `/sys/class/net/<if>/device/msi_irqs` (else `device/irq`, else the names in `/proc/interrupts`), and the CPU of each from `/proc/irq/<n>/effective_affinity_list` (else a single-CPU `smp_affinity_list`; a vector whose CPU those files do not name is skipped, and the interrupt counts are never used to guess it: they are history). The files are world-readable and read once, a bounded number of them, and only when the logger shows a Warn (the rx-port rule reads them too, at planning, and this report reads them again after the loops started, so the two can differ if an interrupt moves between the reads); a file that cannot be read or a format that is not recognised leaves the report empty and nothing fails the start. A container with its own network namespace sees only a virtual interface and finds nothing. A server confined with `taskset` or a cpuset to the big CPUs sees a uniform set, reads nothing and logs nothing, even if a vector of its NIC is serviced by a little CPU. A serviced CPU outside the allowed set is named in the Info line (`nic_irq_cpus`) but not warned about: no loop of this process can run there.

### What was measured

The measurement behind the Warn is one observation on one host, not a guarantee: on a bonded Realtek r8127 arm64 big.LITTLE host, with a TX-completion interrupt serviced by a CPU without a celeris loop, whole-port TX silences of about 200 ms were seen, and they were not seen after the interrupt was moved onto a CPU with a loop. An automatic extra loop on the little CPU was tried and rejected because it raised tail latency on other workloads, so the engine does not add one. Whether another host is affected, or the move helps it, is not known, and why the vector was on CPU 4 after boot is not known either (nothing shows that irqbalance put it there). The interrupt belongs to the host and is shared by every process, so moving it is the operator's choice, outside the process:

### Host tuning

- Move the interrupt onto a loop CPU that services no NIC vector; the Warn lists them (on the host measured, with the vector on CPU 4: 1, 7 and 9-11, not CPU 0, which carries IRQ 159, and not CPUs 6 and 8, which carry the vectors of enp49s0; CPU 1 is the placement that was measured). On 6 or 8 the rx-port rule would keep that CPU's loop at the next start and plan the loops 0, 1, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11, which nobody measured, and two busy vectors would share a CPU. As root: `echo <cpu> > /proc/irq/<irq>/smp_affinity_list`. The setting is reversible (write the old CPU back), is lost on reboot unless made persistent (a boot-time unit or script that repeats the line), and irqbalance can move it again.
- To keep irqbalance from moving it, ban the little CPUs from irqbalance's environment (`/etc/default/irqbalance` or `/etc/sysconfig/irqbalance`, depending on the distribution) and restart it. The variable is `IRQBALANCE_BANNED_CPULIST` (a CPU list) in current irqbalance and `IRQBALANCE_BANNED_CPUS` (a hex mask) in older versions; check the one your irqbalance documents.

### What the warning can miss

A vector is checked only if it carries traffic: in the one read of `/proc/interrupts` made at start (nothing is sampled and nothing sleeps) it must have taken at least 1000 interrupts since boot and at least 5% of the interrupts since boot of all the vectors of the listener's physical NICs (every vector of every port of a bond, whether or not its CPU is known); the one exception is a vector the rx-port rule stepped aside over, which counts at its floor of 1000 whatever its share. On the host measured the four busy vectors of the bond took 14% to 35% each and two others 3 interrupts, so any share from 0.00000003% up to 14% separates them; 5% and 1000 sit well inside that gap and are a choice, not a measurement. A vector with no interrupt, or under the floor or the share, is skipped and never warned about: the Info line counts them (`nic_irq_skipped`: 60 on that host, 58 that never fired and 2 with 3 interrupts) and a Debug line names the ones that took interrupts, with their CPU and count. Only the first 4096 vectors found are judged and counted. The Info line also carries `nic_irq_cpus` (the serviced CPUs) and `irq_took` (the time the reads took). The counts are since boot and the kernel prints them as 32-bit per CPU, which leaves blind spots in both directions. Missing a Warn: a server started at boot will most likely see every vector under 1000 interrupts and log nothing, while a restart later would; a counter that wrapped on the busy vector that matters reads small and can fall under 5% (it is still warned about when the rx-port rule stepped aside over it, but not where the rule does not run, such as an explicit `Workers` or a default count under the non-little CPUs, nor when it fell under 1000); a NIC balanced over more than about 20 evenly busy vectors puts each under 5% and gets no Warn (its Debug reason reads `none reaches the share/floor`). Warning about a vector that does not matter now: a vector that was busy long ago and is quiet now still qualifies, and a counter that wrapped on a busy vector of a big CPU shrinks the total and can lift another vector over 5%. If `/proc/interrupts` or the vectors of one port cannot be read, no vector is judged and nothing is logged beyond a Debug line. The report is made once, at start: an interrupt moved later (irqbalance, an interface restart, a reboot) is not followed, and a server started before its NIC is up (order it `After=network-online.target`) sees no vectors. The adaptive engine reports for each of its two engines when it builds them, the second at its first switch, possibly minutes later; the identical Warn (same logger, address and CPUs) is logged once and the repeat is a Debug line. There is no switch to turn the check off.
